01

Who actually pays for it

The money map — and why federal appropriations are a second-order variable for revenue.

The Congressional Budget Office's Public Spending on Transportation and Water Infrastructure, 1956 to 2023 is the authoritative long-run series. Total public spending on transportation and water infrastructure reached $626bn in 2023 — about 2.3% of GDP — of which water utilities took roughly 28%. Within water, state and local governments fund 92%, and around 96% of water supply and wastewater treatment facilities specifically.

Two consequences follow, and they govern everything downstream.

  1. Washington sets rules far more powerfully than it sets budgets. A lead pipe mandate moves more distributor volume than a grant programme of the same nominal size.
  2. There is no single appropriations line to track. The real budget is 50 states, ~50,000 community water systems and ~17,500 wastewater utilities, funded by rates and municipal debt.

The variable that actually matters

A single year of national water and sewer rate increases generates more incremental utility spending capacity than the entire annual IIJA water supplemental. Bills rose 5.1% in 2025 and 24.2% cumulatively over five years across 50 cities — outpacing inflation, with water up 6.0% and wastewater up 4.8% year on year.

There is also a loss that funds its own repair. Roughly 19.5% of treated drinking water — about 6.75 billion gallons a day — never reaches a paying customer, costing utilities $6.4bn a year in uncaptured revenue. Every mile replaced converts lost water into billable revenue, with a payback computable at the utility level and no grant money required. It is why metering and leak detection grow faster than the pipe business itself.

The EPA surveys are floors by construction: they count only what states could document as eligible, and their baseline assumes a 0.5%/year pipe replacement rate — which imputes a 200-year pipe life against an observed 53-year average failure age. The ASCE and AWWA figures are ceilings by construction, produced by parties that advocate for higher appropriations; AWWA's headline requires a 168% increase in annual capital investment and concedes household bills would roughly double in real terms.

Escalate the two EPA surveys to 2026 dollars at ~4.5% construction inflation and you get ~$76bn/yr of documented eligible need against ~$85–90bn/yr of actual construction put in place. On a like-for-like basis the sector is roughly meeting its documented need. It falls short only against the broader definitions.

The IIJA cliff, in four steps

  1. Gross step-down: $8.7bn/yr. Annual SRF funding went from ~$2.7bn in FY2021 to ~$11.4bn across FY2022–26 — 9.9% of the ~$88bn national base.
  2. The pipeline is not empty. Roughly two-thirds of the ~$50bn — some $33bn — had still to reach municipalities as of mid-2026, spreading the drawdown across 2027–2030.
  3. A large share revolves. On a ~50/50 grant-versus-loan split, ~$25bn of permanent corpus supports ~$2bn/yr of lending in perpetuity. Net steady-state loss: ~$6.7bn/yr, or 7.6% of the base.
  4. Rate growth swamps it. 5.1% on a far larger revenue base more than offsets the federal withdrawal.

The two line items that are legally, not economically, driven

These carry the highest conviction because they are court-enforced deadlines rather than budget preferences.

Lead service lines

9.2 million lines at $4,700–$6,930 each on EPA's estimate — a national total of $45–60bn on consensus figures, against $15bn of dedicated IIJA funding. Inventories, replacement plans and school lists are due 1 November 2027; full replacement is mandated within ten years regardless of measured lead levels. The binary risk: AWWA v. EPA, with oral argument expected in autumn 2026, challenging replacement of lines "under the control" of the utility.

PFAS

EPA's 18 May 2026 proposal retains PFOA and PFOS at 4 ppt but would let systems request an extension to 26 April 2031, and would rescind the MCLs for PFHxS, PFNA, GenX and the Hazard Index on legal and procedural grounds rather than on the health science. Sector cost remains >$50bn over 20 years, but the opportunity is smaller and later than the 2024 rule implied. Any model built on the full six-compound rule is stale.

03

Why it actually needs replacing

The engineering case, the counter-evidence, and an honest verdict on both.

The US operates ~2.2 million miles of drinking water transmission and distribution main across ~50,000 community water systems, plus 800,000+ miles of public sewer and 17,544 publicly owned treatment works serving 82% of the population. Water mains have an observed average failure age of 53 years; treatment plants have a 40–50 year design life, meaning the enormous 1972 Clean Water Act cohort is now at end of life. Plants run at an average 81% of design capacity, with 15% at or over.

Pipe went in the ground in three waves — turn-of-the-century cast iron, post-war cast iron and asbestos cement, then 1970s–80s ductile iron and PVC. Each hits its replacement window in sequence, which is the structural reason renewal spend compounds rather than spikes.

Materials do not fail equally

This is where "aging infrastructure" stops being a slogan. Failure rates differ by an order of magnitude, and the difference is the defensible core of the replacement thesis.

Why cast iron fails: graphitic corrosion

Grey cast iron is an iron matrix laced with graphite flakes. Buried in soil, an electrochemical cell forms between the cathodic graphite and the anodic iron matrix. The iron leaches away; the graphite skeleton remains, preserving the pipe's shape while hollowing out its strength.

The pipe looks intact. It holds its geometry and holds pressure — until a ground movement, pressure transient, freeze cycle or traffic load applies what the graphitised wall can no longer carry, and it fails catastrophically and without warning.

Sulfate soils, de-icing chlorides, sulfate-reducing bacteria and soft water all accelerate it. Soil corrosivity, not calendar age, is the dominant predictor — which is why identical- vintage mains in different soils have wildly different histories, and why blanket age-based replacement is economically inefficient.

The second problem: tuberculation

Bacteria interacting with the iron wall build rough iron-oxide tubercles inside the pipe. Effective diameter shrinks, raising pumping costs and cutting fire-flow capacity sometimes below code; biofilm harbours in the tubercles and consumes disinfectant residual; and under-deposit corrosion continues beneath. A main can be hydraulically obsolete long before it is structurally unsafe — which is a genuine, under-discussed reason utilities replace pipe that has never broken.

The evidence that cuts against the consensus

Presented in full, because the request was for unbiased information — and this case is stronger than the industry generally acknowledges.

Verdict

The US does not need to replace 2.2 million miles of pipe. It needs to replace the ~770,000 miles over 50 years old, prioritised by material and soil corrosivity — and it is doing so at roughly half the required pace for that subset. The replacement case is real, but narrower and slower than the advocacy figures suggest, and through 2030 it is regulation-driven rather than failure-driven. Corroding pipe is a deferrable expense. A consent decree is not.

The genuinely new variable

US data centre water consumption of 17–19 billion gallons a year is projected to reach 60–110 billion by 2030, and 97% of it comes from municipal systems. The infrastructure consequence matters more than the volume: water systems are sized on population growth, and a single hyperscale campus can exceed its host utility's entire planned growth allowance — forcing transmission upsizing, storage, pumping and often dedicated treatment. Three announcements in February 2026 alone carried water infrastructure costs approaching $1bn. The politics are a live risk, since those costs usually land on residential ratepayers rather than the developer.

Together the two are $14.2–14.8bn against the $39bn US TAM Core & Main cites — a combined 36–38% share of a market both describe as fragmented. That is a duopoly at the head of a long tail, and it is why both have been able to consolidate and hold pricing. It also cross-checks: 20% of $39bn is $7.8bn, against CNM's reported $7.65bn.

The sensitivity most investors get wrong

A 100bp change in municipal market growth is worth $34m of revenue to Core & Main — 0.44% of total net sales, about $4m of EBITDA — and $71m to Ferguson, or 0.23% of group revenue. Even at full effect the IIJA drawdown is worth well under a percentage point of revenue growth to either company. Residential construction — 18% of CNM's mix and about half of Ferguson's US mix — moves both far more.

What management says, and whether it checks out

Core & Main was asked directly on 10 June 2026. The answer is the most specific public account of where the IIJA money physically sits.

"On IIJA, the remaining funding is expected to hit the state revolving funds this year. That doesn't mean that there's any cliff to the funding or there's any end to the funding… Only about a third or less of it has hit the municipality level yet. … A portion of that funding is in the grant form, and then the other portion is in low interest loans. A portion of that would get repaid back into those state revolving funds." Brad Cowles, President, Core & Main — Q1 FY2026 earnings call
"95% of the funding that municipalities use for their water infrastructure is state and local… We're seeing municipalities increase water rates to their customers to be able to afford some of the upgrades. We're seeing municipal bond growth and the municipal bond issuance that's going out there." Robyn Bradbury, CFO, Core & Main — Q1 FY2026 earnings call

Both claims verify independently — CBO puts the state and local share at 92–96%, and Bluefield measures 2025 rate growth at 5.1%. This is the rare case where management commentary and third-party data agree, which raises the confidence weighting.

Neither is growing at the market rate

Both are growing within the market by moving into more technical categories. Core & Main's treatment plant and smart utility / AMI lines have compounded at 15–25% over five years, against low-single-digit growth for the whole business; treatment plant is a mid-single-digit percentage of net sales and management calls it "non-discretionary… a pretty durable long-term trend." Ferguson is playing the same demand pool through large capital projects — mid- to high-single-digit percentage of revenue and trending up, with backlogs building above realised growth rates including waterworks, and the $1.6bn FloWorks acquisition explicitly justified on cross-selling into waterworks, commercial/mechanical and industrial.

One overlay that distorts the reported numbers

PVC deflation is currently masking municipal volume strength at both companies. Core & Main delivered flat revenue with +50bps of gross margin and +6% adjusted EPS — a volume-and-mix story running underneath a price headwind. Ferguson reported PVC "down about in the double-digit range for the quarter" with commodities ~15% of revenue. If announced resin-driven increases stick, reported growth accelerates with no change in underlying demand. If they do not, the reverse.

05

What would change this view

Synthesis, and the caveats that belong on the front page rather than the last.

  • The market is mid-single-digit nominal growth — replacement-led, low-cyclicality, regulation-anchored. It is not a boom and will not be one. Anyone underwriting a step-function infrastructure cycle is relying on numbers that will not be spent.
  • Core & Main is the higher-beta pure play. 44% municipal, 100% water, 50% repair-and-replace. Its FY26 guidance of +2–3% sits below the market growth the model implies, which is a low bar if municipal strength persists and PVC pricing normalises.
  • Ferguson is the diluted expression. Waterworks is ~23% of group revenue and decelerated to +3% on a +15% comparable. A feature if you want the infrastructure theme without municipal-budget risk; a bug if you want a clean water proxy.
  • The durable earnings driver is consolidation, not the market's growth rate — M&A and category expansion into treatment and metering, from a combined 36–38% share of a fragmented market.
Caveats that matter

Verify the public-sector figures against primary documents before relying on them. This analysis was assembled under network restrictions that blocked direct retrieval from cbo.gov, epa.gov, census.gov and fred.stlouisfed.org, so those numbers came via search results summarising the primary sources rather than from the source PDFs. Company financials do not carry this caveat — they came from Quartr's primary filings and transcripts.

Other known soft spots: Ferguson's waterworks revenue is estimated from a seasonally strong quarter applied to an annualised US base — Ferguson does not report it. The ~50/50 IIJA grant-versus-loan split driving the revolving offset is an assumption. The $88bn base bundles Census categories that are not a clean match for municipal water and wastewater capital spending; rebuilding that series from FRED is the highest-value next step. And the forward model is a framework for bounding outcomes, not a forecast with a claim to precision — the ~32% bear-to-bull spread by 2035 is the honest measure of confidence.

This contains no valuation, no price target and no recommendation. It sizes a market, models its trajectory, and tests the engineering claims underneath it.
