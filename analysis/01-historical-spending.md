# 1. Historical Spending on US Water Infrastructure

*Federal, state and municipal — and how each dollar reaches a distributor's income statement.*

---

## 1.1 The single most important structural fact

**State and local governments pay for essentially all of it.**

The Congressional Budget Office's *Public Spending on Transportation and Water
Infrastructure, 1956 to 2023* (published February 2025) is the authoritative long-run
series. Its findings for water:

| Metric | Value | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total public spending, all transportation + water infrastructure, 2023 | **$626bn** | ~2.3% of GDP |
| Water utilities — total spending, 2023 | **$175bn** (~28% of the total) | Second only to highways |
| — of which **capital** | **$59bn** | The figure that drives materials demand |
| — of which **operations & maintenance** | **$114bn** | 65% of utility spending is running the system, not building it |
| State + local share of water infrastructure spending | **92%** | Federal share 8% |
| State + local share of water supply & wastewater treatment facilities specifically | **~96%** | Narrower capital-facilities definition |
| State + local share of **all** public infrastructure spending, 2023 | **79%** ($494.2bn of $625.8bn) | |
| Operations & maintenance as a share of all public infrastructure spending, 2023 | **56.7%** ($355bn of $625.8bn) | Rising over time; capital is the minority |

**The $59bn capital line is the number that matters for a distributor**, and it is the anchor
for the forward model in [§2.2](02-forward-budget.md#22-sizing-the-actual-capital-base--four-routes-to-the-same-number).
Escalated to 2026 it implies a ~$67bn national water capital base — well below the ~$91bn a
naive reading of the Census construction series suggests.

Two consequences follow directly, and they govern everything else in this analysis:

1. **Federal policy is a second-order variable for revenue.** Washington sets *rules*
   (which create mandatory spending) far more powerfully than it sets *budgets*. A lead
   pipe mandate moves more distributor volume than a grant program of the same nominal size.
2. **The relevant "budget" is 50 states × ~50,000 community water systems × ~17,500
   wastewater utilities**, funded by rates and municipal debt. There is no single
   appropriations line to track.

Core & Main's CFO put the same number on the Q1 FY2026 earnings call: *"95% of the funding
that municipalities use for their water infrastructure is state and local."* That is not
company spin — it matches CBO.

## 1.2 The construction spending series — the highest-frequency hard data

The US Census Bureau's *Value of Construction Put in Place* (C30) is the closest thing to
a monthly read on water infrastructure demand. Two categories matter:

| Census category | Latest reading (Jan 2026, seasonally adjusted annual rate) |
| --- | --- |
| Total construction, water supply | **$36.5bn** |
| Public construction, water supply | **$34.8bn** |
| Total construction, sewage & waste disposal | **$54.3bn** |
| Public construction, sewage & waste disposal | **~$53bn** |

Combined, that is roughly **$91bn/year of water-related construction put in place**, of which
the public sector is ~95%. **Do not use this as the water capital base.** "Sewage and waste
disposal" bundles solid-waste facilities — landfill, waste-to-energy — with sewerage, and the
series includes private work. Adjusting for both brings it to ~$68bn, which is where CBO's
capital line independently lands. The full reconciliation is in
[§2.2](02-forward-budget.md#22-sizing-the-actual-capital-base--four-routes-to-the-same-number).

For longer-run context, the US Conference of Mayors' *2025 Public Infrastructure Spending*
report puts cumulative 2012–2024 local capital spending at **$350bn on sewer** and
**$226bn on water supply** — about $27bn and $17bn per year respectively on a
local-government-only basis, within total public community-asset capital spending that
grew 76%, from $279bn (2012) to $492bn (2024).

**Reading for distributors:** this series is the numerator that CNM and FERG's waterworks
volumes track, with a lag of roughly two to four quarters between construction put in
place and materials purchase. It has grown steadily and has not, to date, shown an
IIJA-driven spike — consistent with federal money being marginal.

## 1.3 The federal channels, sized

There are four federal pipes into the sector. In descending order of relevance:

### (a) The State Revolving Funds (SRFs) — the core program

Two revolving loan funds, capitalized by annual federal grants to states, which then lend
to utilities and recycle repayments:

| Program | Pre-IIJA baseline (FY2021) | FY2025 regular appropriation | FY2025 IIJA supplemental |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clean Water SRF (wastewater) | ~$1.64bn total available | $1.6bn | +$2.6bn |
| Drinking Water SRF | — | $1.1bn | +$2.6bn |

Regular appropriations for EPA's water infrastructure programs have been **flat across
FY2024, FY2025 and FY2026**. All growth came from the supplemental.

### (b) The IIJA / Bipartisan Infrastructure Law supplemental — expiring

The 2021 law made **over $50bn available for water and wastewater over FY2022–FY2026**,
split across five pots:

| Pot | Amount (FY2022–26) |
| --- | --- |
| Clean Water SRF general supplemental | $11.7bn |
| Drinking Water SRF general supplemental | $11.7bn |
| DWSRF — lead service line replacement | $15.0bn |
| DWSRF — emerging contaminants (PFAS) | $4.0bn |
| CWSRF emerging contaminants + other | balance to >$50bn |

The National League of Cities frames the aggregate effect precisely: IIJA **quadrupled
annual SRF funding from ~$2.7bn (FY2021) to ~$11.4bn (FY2022–26)**. That supplemental
authority **expires 30 September 2026**. See [§2.4](02-forward-budget.md#24-the-iija-cliff-arithmetic-done-honestly)
for the arithmetic on what that actually costs the sector.

### (c) WIFIA — federal credit, not federal cash

The Water Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act program lends at Treasury rates for
large projects. As of May 2026 it had financed **$23bn across 152 closed loans, supporting
$51bn of total project value**, with roughly **$11bn of capacity still available**. Recent
run-rate is modest — 8 loans / ~$1.2bn in FY2025, 3 loans / $682m in early 2026 — and EPA
has been waiving fees to widen access for small communities.

WIFIA is leverage, not subsidy: it lowers the cost of capital on projects utilities were
largely going to build anyway. Treat it as a mild accelerant.

### (d) Congressionally directed spending (earmarks)

Community Project Funding is carved **out of** the SRF appropriation rather than added to
it, so it reallocates rather than expands. The FY2027 House bill contains nearly **1,200
drinking water and wastewater earmarks totalling more than $1bn** — which, against a
proposed 16% SRF cut, means the formula-driven capitalization grants to states shrink twice
over.

## 1.4 The real funding engine: rates and municipal debt

Because federal money is 4–8% of the total, the variables that actually set distributor
demand are household water bills and municipal bond issuance.

**Rates are inflecting up and outpacing inflation.** Bluefield Research's US Municipal
Water & Sewer Rate Index (50 cities):

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Combined water + sewer bill increase, 2025 | **+5.1%** (five-year high) |
| Cumulative five-year increase | **+24.2%** |
| Water rates, 2024→2025 | +6.0% |
| Wastewater rates, 2024→2025 | +4.8% |

Individual 2026 increases run well above that: Portland +6.4% on a typical bill, San
Francisco ~12.5–12.6% per year, Birmingham MI +20% water / +14% sewer.

**Municipal debt is the capital vehicle.** Water and sewer revenue bonds financed
$27.9bn–$39.9bn/year even in the low-rate 2010–2013 period, and issuers who deferred during
the 2023–24 rate spike have been returning to market. Core & Main's CFO explicitly flagged
"municipal bond growth and the municipal bond issuance that's going out there" as a
funding source she watches.

**Why this matters more than the federal debate:** a 5% national rate increase on a
~$130bn+ annual water and sewer revenue base generates more incremental utility spending
capacity in a single year than the entire annual IIJA water supplemental.

## 1.5 A structural loss that funds itself

Roughly **19.5% of treated drinking water — about 6.75 billion gallons per day — never
reaches a paying customer**, costing utilities **$6.4bn/year in uncaptured revenue**
(Bluefield Research). Five states — California, Texas, Florida, New York and Illinois —
account for more than a third of national losses.

This is the cleanest economic argument for pipe replacement that does not depend on grant
money: every mile replaced converts lost water into billable revenue, and the payback is
computable at the utility level. It is also why metering and leak detection (Core & Main's
"smart utility" line) grow faster than the pipe business itself.

## 1.6 How the money map translates into distributor revenue

| Funding layer | Annual scale | Volatility | Passes through to CNM/FERG as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Utility rates & O&M budgets | Largest; ~$175bn incl. O&M | Very low | Repair & replace, break repairs, meters, everyday MRO |
| Municipal revenue bonds | $30–40bn+ | Moderate (rate-sensitive) | Large capital projects, treatment plant builds |
| SRF loans (state-recycled) | ~$11bn during IIJA; ~$2.7bn base federal | Low — revolving corpus persists | Mid-size mains, plant upgrades |
| IIJA supplemental grants | ~$8.7bn/yr incremental, ending FY2026 | **High — expiring** | LSL replacement, PFAS treatment |
| WIFIA credit | ~$1–2bn/yr closings | Low | Very large single projects |
| Federal earmarks | ~$1bn, reallocated | Political | Named local projects |

Core & Main reports **50% of net sales from repair & replace vs 50% new construction**,
with **44% municipal / 38% non-residential / 18% residential** end-market mix (fiscal year
ended 1 February 2026). The repair-and-replace half is funded almost entirely from the
first row of that table — rates and O&M — which is the least cyclical money in the sector.
