# 3. Technical Drivers — Why the Infrastructure Actually Needs Replacement

*The engineering case, the counter-evidence, and an honest verdict.*

---

## 3.1 The asset base

| Asset | Scale |
| --- | --- |
| Community water systems | ~50,000 (of 148,000+ public water systems total) |
| Drinking water transmission & distribution mains | **~2.2 million miles** |
| Public sewers | **800,000+ miles**, plus ~500,000 miles of private laterals |
| Publicly owned treatment works (POTWs) | **17,544**, serving 270.4m people (82% of population) |
| POTW utilisation | Average **81% of design capacity**; **15% at or over capacity** |
| Lead service lines | **9.2 million** |
| Combined sewer communities | ~860, discharging **~850 billion gallons/yr** of untreated wastewater |

Two design-life facts frame everything:

- **Water mains: observed average failure age of 53 years** (Utah State University, 2023 —
  the largest survey of its kind, covering ~400,000 miles across the US and Canada).
- **Treatment plants: 40–50 year design life; process equipment 15–20 years.** Plants built
  around the 1972 Clean Water Act — the largest cohort ever constructed — are now at or past
  end of life.

The US installed pipe in three great waves: the late 19th/early 20th century (cast iron, in
the oldest Northeastern and Midwestern cities), the post-WWII suburban expansion (cast iron
then asbestos cement), and the 1970s–80s (ductile iron, then PVC). Each wave is now hitting
its replacement window roughly in sequence, which is the structural reason the sector's
renewal spend compounds rather than spikes.

## 3.2 Failure mechanisms, by material

This is where "aging infrastructure" stops being a slogan and becomes physics. Materials do
not degrade uniformly, and the observed failure rates differ by an order of magnitude.

### Annual break rates per 100 miles of main (Utah State University, 2023)

| Material | Breaks / 100 mi / yr | Share of installed mains > 50 yrs old |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cast iron** | **28.6** | **86%** |
| Asbestos cement | 10.3 | 41% |
| Steel | 9.2 | — |
| Ductile iron | 5.1 | — |
| **PVC** | **2.9** | — |
| **National average** | **11.1** | 33% of all mains (~770,000 miles) |

**Cast iron fails at roughly ten times the rate of PVC.** That single ratio, more than any
aggregate spending gap, is the defensible core of the replacement thesis.

### Why cast iron fails: graphitic corrosion

Grey cast iron is an iron matrix laced with graphite flakes. Buried in soil, an
electrochemical cell forms between the **cathodic graphite flakes and the anodic iron
matrix**. The iron leaches away as corrosion product; the graphite skeleton remains,
preserving the pipe's outward shape while hollowing out its strength. Chlorides accumulate
at the corrosion interface within graphitic pits.

The engineering consequence is what makes this asset class treacherous:

> The pipe *looks* intact. It retains its geometry and holds pressure — until a ground
> movement, a pressure transient, a freeze cycle or traffic loading applies the load the
> graphitised wall can no longer carry, and it fails catastrophically and without warning.

Aggravating conditions: soils containing sulfates, chlorides from de-icing salt, sulfate-
reducing bacteria, acidic or soft water, and salt-water intrusion. **Soil corrosivity, not
calendar age, is the dominant predictor** — which is why identical-vintage mains in
different soils have wildly different failure histories, and why blanket age-based
replacement programmes are economically inefficient.

### The second cast-iron problem: tuberculation

Bacteria interacting with the iron wall build rough iron-oxide tubercles on the pipe
interior. Three consequences, all costly:

1. **Hydraulic capacity loss** — the effective diameter shrinks, raising pumping energy costs
   and reducing fire-flow capacity, sometimes below code.
2. **Water quality degradation** — tubercles harbour biofilm, consume disinfectant residual,
   and cause discoloured-water complaints.
3. **Accelerated structural loss** — under-deposit corrosion continues beneath the tubercle.

A tuberculated main can be hydraulically obsolete long before it is structurally unsafe.
This is a genuine, under-discussed driver: utilities replace mains that have not broken.

### Asbestos cement

Roughly 41% of installed AC pipe is now over 50 years old. AC degrades by leaching of the
cement matrix (softening from both internal soft water and external aggressive soils),
losing wall strength progressively. It breaks at 10.3 per 100 miles/yr — better than cast
iron, worse than everything else. It also carries handling, worker-safety and disposal cost
premiums on replacement, which raises the unit cost of an AC replacement programme above a
like-for-like cast iron programme.

### The materials transition is already visible in the data

Cast iron and asbestos cement together were **41% of installed mains in 2018 and 33% in
2023** — an ~8 point reduction in five years. The replacement cycle is not hypothetical; it
is measurably underway, and it is systematically substituting high-failure materials for
low-failure ones.

## 3.3 What failure actually costs

| Consequence | Magnitude |
| --- | --- |
| Water main breaks | **>260,000/yr** across the US and Canada — roughly one every two minutes |
| Treated water lost | **19.5% of production; ~6.75 billion gallons/day** |
| Lost revenue | **$6.4bn/yr** |
| Water main funding shortfall (USU, 2024) | **$452bn** across the US and Canada |
| CSO discharge | **~850 billion gallons/yr** of untreated wastewater from ~860 communities |

Note the asymmetry: the direct financial cost of a break (repair crew, pavement, lost water)
is modest, while the indirect costs (boil-water notices, service interruption, road closure,
third-party damage, consent-decree exposure) are large but diffuse and rarely capitalised
into a utility's replacement decision. **This is the core reason under-investment persists:
the costs of deferral are externalised and the costs of replacement are on the rate base.**

## 3.4 The evidence that cuts *against* the consensus

The user asked for unbiased information. Here is the case for the sector being less
distressed than the headline numbers suggest. It is stronger than the industry generally
acknowledges.

### (a) Break rates are falling, not rising

The USU study — the same study cited to justify the $452bn shortfall — found the national
break rate **fell from 14.0 to 11.1 breaks per 100 miles per year between 2018 and 2023,
a ~20% decline**. Break rates for the worst materials fell because those materials are being
removed from the network. Whatever the funding gap is, **the network is measurably getting
more reliable, not less**. Reporting that presents a rising-failure narrative is not
supported by the best available dataset.

### (b) Demand is declining, so capacity need is not growing

USGS data: average per capita withdrawal fell from **~140 to ~127 gallons/capita/day between
2000 and 2020**; public-supply domestic per capita use fell from **88 gpd (2010) to 82 gpd
(2015)**; total US withdrawals fell **9% from 2010 to 2015** to 322 bgd, continuing a steady
decline since 2005. Public-supply withdrawals declined even as population grew 4%.

Implication: **the sector's spending need is almost entirely renewal, not expansion.** New
capacity arguments (outside data-centre and Sun Belt growth corridors) are weak. Some
existing infrastructure is genuinely oversized for current demand, and right-sizing on
replacement is a legitimate cost saving that headline "replace like-for-like" estimates
ignore.

### (c) The needs surveys have documented upward and structural biases

- **GAO-24-106251** found the CWSRF is allocated using a **formula from 1987** that does not
  reflect current state population or needs, and criticised EPA's needs-estimation method.
- **EPA's own Office of Inspector General** found the 7th DWINSA's design and execution "did
  not result in allotments of lead service line funds that accurately reflected the LSL
  replacement needs in each state."
- **States have a direct financial incentive to report high needs**, because the surveys
  drive the allotment formula. This is an unavoidable structural bias in a
  self-reported, allocation-determining survey.
- Conversely, the surveys **undercount** distribution and transmission, because they only
  count documented plans and most systems have none.

The biases run in both directions. The correct posture is that these are **allocation
instruments that happen to be quoted as needs estimates**, and they should not be treated as
capital budgets.

### (d) The 200-year pipe fallacy — but read it carefully

DWINSA's baseline assumes a **0.5%/yr replacement rate, implying a 200-year pipe life**,
against an observed **53-year average failure age**. This looks like a devastating
inconsistency, and it is often cited as one. It is more subtle than that:

- Average *failure* age is not average *service* life. Pipes that fail are a biased sample:
  the worst material in the worst soil fails first. Well-bedded PVC and cement-lined ductile
  iron in benign soils routinely exceed 100 years.
- But 0.5%/yr is still demonstrably too slow for the cast-iron and AC cohorts specifically,
  which is where 33% of the network and the overwhelming majority of failures sit.

**The defensible conclusion is targeted, not universal:** the US does not need to replace
2.2 million miles of pipe. It needs to replace the ~770,000 miles that are over 50 years old,
prioritised by material and soil corrosivity — and it is currently doing so at roughly half
the required pace for that subset.

### (e) "Run to failure" is sometimes the correct engineering answer

For small-diameter mains in non-critical service in benign soils, reactive repair has a
lower lifecycle cost than proactive replacement. Not every old pipe should be replaced. The
industry's own asset-management literature says this; the advocacy literature does not.
Utilities that adopt condition-based rather than age-based programmes typically replace
*less* pipe, more effectively.

## 3.5 Where the case is strongest: regulation, not decay

The most reliable near-term drivers are legal obligations with dates attached, because they
are not subject to the deferral dynamics described above.

| Driver | Mechanism | Timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lead & Copper Rule Improvements** | 9.2m lead service lines; full replacement mandated **regardless of measured lead levels**; inventories, replacement plans, school/childcare lists due **1 Nov 2027**; replacement complete 2034–2037 | Peak 2028–2033. **Risk: AWWA v. EPA, oral argument expected Fall 2026** |
| **PFAS (PFOA/PFOS at 4 ppt)** | Retained in EPA's 18 May 2026 proposal; compliance deadline proposed to slip to **26 Apr 2031** on request; four other PFAS MCLs proposed for rescission | Capital shifts to 2029–2031; opportunity **smaller than the 2024 rule implied** |
| **CSO / SSO consent decrees** | Court-supervised Clean Water Act settlements; ~860 combined-sewer communities; ~850bn gal/yr discharge | Multi-decade, non-deferrable — a court order is not a budget line |
| **POTW end-of-life** | 1972-era plants past 40–50 yr design life; 15% of plants at or over design capacity | Continuous through 2035 |

Lead and PFAS are the two mandates where non-compliance carries enforcement exposure. That
is what makes them the highest-conviction components of the forecast — and it is why
Core & Main's treatment plant and metering lines have compounded at 15–25% while the
overall business grew in low single digits.

## 3.6 The new demand driver: data centres

A genuinely new variable, and one both distributors now cite:

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| US data centre direct water consumption today | 17–19 billion gallons/yr |
| Projected by 2030 | **60–110 billion gallons/yr** |
| Share supplied by municipal systems | **97%** |
| Single large facility withdrawal | >1m gallons/day; some allocations up to 8m gpd |
| Feb 2026 announcements (VA, LA, IN) | Water infrastructure cost approaching **$1bn** for three projects |

The infrastructure consequence matters more than the consumption figure: water systems are
sized on *population* growth, and a single hyperscale campus can exceed the entire planned
growth allowance of its host utility. That forces transmission main upsizing, new storage,
pump station capacity and often dedicated reuse or treatment facilities — precisely the
large-diameter, engineered-project work that carries a distributor's best margins.

The politics are a live risk: these costs frequently land on residential ratepayers rather
than the developer, and the resulting local opposition can delay or block projects.

---

## 3.7 Verdict

**The replacement case is real, but it is narrower and slower than the advocacy figures
suggest, and it is regulation-driven rather than failure-driven.**

What is well-supported by evidence:
- ~770,000 miles of main are over 50 years old; cast iron fails ~10x more often than PVC;
  86% of cast iron is past 50 years.
- Graphitic corrosion produces catastrophic, unwarned failure — a genuine safety case.
- 1972-vintage treatment plants are at end of design life, and 15% of plants are at or over
  design capacity.
- 9.2m lead service lines carry a hard legal replacement mandate.
- ~19.5% water loss has a computable, self-funding replacement payback.

What is not well-supported:
- Any claim that the network is deteriorating *faster* — break rates fell 20% in five years.
- Capacity-expansion arguments — per capita demand has fallen for two decades.
- Treating EPA needs surveys, ASCE gaps or AWWA totals as capital budgets. They are
  advocacy and allocation instruments produced by parties with a stated interest in higher
  appropriations, and they should be quoted with that attribution.

The investable conclusion: **this is a mid-single-digit-growth, low-cyclicality,
regulation-anchored replacement market — not a step-function infrastructure boom.** Anyone
underwriting the latter is relying on numbers that will not be spent.
